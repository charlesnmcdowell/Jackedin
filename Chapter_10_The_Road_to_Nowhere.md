# Chapter 10: The Road to Nowhere

### The Celebration

The "Iron Skillet" diner just outside of Denver smelled of bacon grease and stale hope. It was 1:00 AM. Marcus sat in a booth by the window, staring at the Argus-5 parked under the amber floodlights.

It was scarred. A long, silver gash ran down the side of the trailer where he’d kissed the barrier. The front bumper was cracked. But it was standing.

He ordered a steak and eggs. He told himself he was celebrating. He had beaten the storm. He had beaten the odds. He was still standing.

"Hell of a night out there," the waitress said, pouring him a cup of coffee that looked like motor oil.

"Kept it between the ditches," Marcus said, forcing a grin. "That's all that matters."

He checked his bank app. The $305.00 deposit sat at the top of the ledger, a tiny, insulting spark of green in a sea of red. His emergency stash was still hidden in the truck—five thousand dollars of "run-away" money that represented the only part of him the bank hadn't figured out how to digitize yet. The payout from the haul had been intercepted before it even cleared, instantly rerouted to cover the past-due loan and the software subscription.

But he was alive. And he had his family. He’d make the next run. He’d double down. The math would work eventually.

### The Gossip

A heavy man with a red beard slid into the booth across the aisle. Big Mike. An old road dog from the analog days.

"Marcus Turner," Mike boomed. "I saw your rig out there. Looks like you went ten rounds with a guardrail."

"Ice on the pass," Marcus said, cutting into his steak. "She held up."

"Better than most," Mike said. "I saw three autonomous units in the ditch near Vail. They just locked up and slid. Computers don't know how to drift."

Mike laughed, pulling out his phone. "Speaking of drifting... look at this dash-cam footage. Three Waymos piled up like firewood. It's trending on LiveLeak."

He turned the screen toward Marcus. The video showed the autonomous trucks sliding helplessly into a ditch, but Marcus’s eyes drifted to the sidebar—the "Recommended for You" ads pulsing with neon colors.

*LONELY? REAL CONNECTION. REAL GIRLS.*

One of the thumbnails caught his eye. A white BMW. A pink wig. A face that Marcus knew better than his own.

He froze. The steak turned to ash in his mouth.

"Mike," Marcus said, his voice a low rumble. "Scroll down. That ad. Click it."

"What? The cam-girl site?" Mike grinned. "Didn't take you for a digital man, Marcus."

"Just click it."

Mike tapped the thumbnail. The profile for "The Real K" loaded.

It was Keisha.

She wasn't in a studio. She was in a Motel 6 room that looked exactly like the ones Marcus stayed in. She was wearing a cheap, neon-pink wig and... nothing else. She looked tired. Her eyes were dead, staring past the camera lens at a ring light that reflected in her pupils like tiny, artificial suns.

The caption read: *The Influencer game is dead. Reality is expensive. $20 for the real me.*

"That ain't her," Marcus said, his voice a low rumble. He gripped the fork so hard his knuckles turned white. "Just some girl."

"If you say so," Mike shrugged, scrolling down. "Hell of a resemblance though."

Marcus stood up. He threw a twenty-dollar bill on the table—one of the few he had left in his pocket. He walked out of the diner, leaving the steak untouched.

### The Call

He sat in the cab of the Argus, the heater humming. He felt sick. The life he’d built for his kids—the "stable" tech job, the "glamorous" influencer life—it was all just a different floor of the same basement. 

He dialed Junior. He needed to hear a voice that made sense. He needed to hear that at least one of them was still winning.

"Hey, Pop?" Junior’s voice was barely a whisper.

"Junior," Marcus said, his own voice shaking. "I... I had a rough night. But I made the run. Listen, I need to know you're good. How's the coding gig? How's the portfolio?"

Silence. Long, heavy silence.

"Pop... I'm not coding anymore."

"What? You took a vacation?"

"I got laid off, Pop. It wasn't even a person. They just updated the repo. I woke up and my access was revoked. The bot does my whole week's work in four seconds. My stock portfolio is still there, but I can't touch it without penalties, and it's not enough to live on anyway. The liquidity is locked up."

"So what are you doing?" Marcus asked, watching the snow fall on the cracked windshield of his truck.

"I'm driving," Junior said. "Uber. Lyft. DoorDash. Whatever the algorithm throws me. I'm in the Camry twelve hours a day, Pop. Just trying to keep the lights on in the condo until I figure it out."

Marcus closed his eyes. 

The weight of the gym bags he’d dropped on Julian’s desk eighteen hours ago felt like it was still in his hands—the phantom weight of twenty years of work. He’d burned it all to save his identity, to stay an owner, to make sure his kids would never have to sweat like he did.

And here they were.

The tech genius was driving a Camry for an algorithm. The influencer was selling a ghost of herself for twenty bucks a pop. And Marcus Turner, the Last Bull, was a sharecropper in a three-hundred-thousand-dollar cage, risking his life on black ice to pay for the right to keep working.

They hadn't "won." They hadn't "escaped." The house hadn't even broken a sweat; it had just waited for them to exhaust themselves, then offered them a seat at the table they were currently serving.

### The Last Bull

**Two Years Later.**

The I-10 was a river of silence.

The Ghost Fleets owned the horizon now—long, elegant chains of autonomous steel moving with a precision that made Marcus’s teeth ache. He was the "glitch" they allowed to stay because he took the Dirty Miles, the high-risk loads, the hauls that required a human to sign the liability waiver.

Marcus Turner sat in the driver’s seat of the Argus-5, his left arm resting on the door. He called it a Kenworth to the women he met—a "beast of chrome and sensors"—because saying *Argus* felt like saying the name of his jailer.

His phone buzzed. Maya.

*Coming through tonight? I’m at the warehouse. The new software is a nightmare. I need a distraction.*

Marcus reached into the glovebox, his fingers brushing the stack of bills. It felt thin. The rubber band that used to be a tight coil of security was loose now, rattling against the floor of the compartment. He had thirty-two hundred dollars left to his name.

He thought about the dealership. The way they’d smiled when they handed him the "Restructuring Agreement" after the Vail Pass incident. The manual override had voided his insurance for the trip. The cost of the repairs—the sensors, the trailer skin, the mandatory "Battery Stress Audit"—was billed directly to him. Since he didn't have forty thousand in cash, they’d "kindly" rolled it back into the loan principal, effectively swallowing his down payment. Now, he was a "Contract-Equity Partner."

He looked at the dashboard. *Current Equity: 1.8%*. 

He’d done the math on a napkin last night. At this rate—after the interest, the software lease, and the "liability penalties" they tacked on after every storm—he’d own the title in eighty-two years. He’d be a hundred and twenty-four years old, handing the keys to a ghost.

He shifted into gear, the electric motors emitting a high-pitched, predatory whine. He wasn't the predator anymore. He was the battery.

He tapped a reply.

*On my way. Bringing the beast. See you soon.*

He pulled out into the flow, a man in a cage, driving as fast as the algorithm would let him toward the only friction he had left.
