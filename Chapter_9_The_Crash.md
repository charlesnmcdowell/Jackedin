# Chapter 9: The Near Miss

### The Invisible Tally

The interior of the Argus-5 was a temple of cold efficiency, but Marcus Turner had turned it into a sweatbox.

He had been on the road for thirty-six hours straight, including the grueling six-hour crawl out of the paralyzed San Francisco gridlock. In the Peterbilt, the vibration of the diesel and the fight with the wheel would have broken him by now. The Argus was a silent partner, managing the lane-keeping and the drafting while Marcus satisfied the infrared eye on the A-pillar with a steady, unblinking stare.

He hated the interface. The "Pilot-OS" was a flat, glassy mystery that required him to tap through three menus just to adjust the lumbar support. He’d ignored the mandatory "Vanguard Pilot Training" videos, figuring he’d been driving since Julian was in diapers and didn't need a cartoon to tell him how to turn a wheel. He’d spent the first three days of the haul just trying to figure out how to dim the dashboard lights so they didn't blind him at night.

The dash was a countdown to insolvency.

*Subscription Status: ACTIVE (4 hours remaining). Next Payment: $2,400.00.*
*Loan Principal Installment: $4,500.00 (PAST DUE).*
*Current Load Value: $4,200.00 (On-Time Bonus: $1,500.00).*

The math was a knife. Denver by midnight meant a $5,700 payout—not even enough to cover the month’s overhead, but enough to keep the bank from remotely locking his wheels. A "Safety-Buffer" stop meant the bonus evaporated, the subscription lapsed, and the truck would "Safe-Park" itself—a high-tech repo.

"I can beat it," Marcus whispered. 

He wasn't talking to the screen. He was talking to the hole in his life where his dignity used to be. He checked the side mirror, watching the sleet turn the Rockies into a grey, vertical tomb. The I-70 was no longer a highway; it was a poker table.

### The Override

The Rockies were a wall of white noise. A spring blizzard had turned the climb into a blur of grey slush and black ice.

"Warning," the Argus’s voice was a calm insult. "Traction below safety threshold. Reducing speed to 45 MPH. Re-routing for weather hold."

"No," Marcus growled. "Negative. We’re doing seventy. We have the weight."

"Weather hold is mandatory for Pilot-OS Tier 1 users. Braking sequence initiated."

The truck began to slow. The regenerative braking hummed—a soft, mechanical rejection of his authority.

He fumbled with the screen, his fingers thick and clumsy on the glass. "Override. Manual override! Come on, you piece of junk!"

The screen flashed a "Safety Logic" warning. It wanted a PIN. It wanted a verification. Marcus swore, reaching into the glovebox and pulling out a crumpled, grease-stained post-it note. He’d scribbled a sequence down when he’d seen the tech in Oakland moving a bricked unit in the back lot.

"Alpha... Niner... Niner... enter," he muttered, punching the keys with his thumb. 

"Diagnostic mode enabled," the voice said. "Warning: All safety buffers disabled. Responsibility is 100% manual."

The power returned with a violent, intoxicating surge. In the old Peterbilt, a downshift took seconds of prayer and double-clutching. In the Argus, the four electric motors delivered 100% of their torque the millisecond his foot hit the floor. The rig didn't just move; it leaped. The high-pitched whine of the motors vibrated through Marcus's teeth, a sound of pure, unadulterated power. He grabbed the yoke. He was a man again, not a passenger.

He hit the curve at Vail Pass at sixty-eight miles an hour. 

The Argus was too smooth, its suspension masking the road rather than reporting it. There was no "wiggle" in the seat, no physical warning. By the time the black ice claimed the rear axles, the window for mercy had closed.

The trailer stepped out. The "Jackknife Waltz." 

Marcus felt the sickening slide in his gut. A lesser driver would have slammed the brakes. A panicked driver would have overcorrected.

Marcus did neither. He squinted at the windshield, which was now a high-contrast HUD. The truck’s infrared sensors were punching through the snow, highlighting a three-foot strip of dry asphalt two hundred yards ahead—a detail his own eyes couldn't see in the whiteout.

"I see you," he hissed.

He feathered the throttle, fighting every instinct that screamed *stop*. He used the instant response of the electric motors to pulse the drive wheels, keeping the trailer from swinging past the point of no return. 

The trailer swung wide, scraping the barrier with a shriek of sparks that lit up the snow like fireworks. The truck shuddered, the suspension groaning under the lateral Gs.

But it held. 

The tires found the dry patch the sensors had promised. The rig snapped back into line with a violent lurch that threw Marcus against the door. 

He didn't slow down. He punched the throttle, the electric torque pinning him to the ergonomic leather seat as he blasted through the slush and crest the hill. He looked at the dash, a grim smile touching his lips. He hated the bank, but God, he loved the machine.

### The Hollow Win

He rolled into the Denver distribution center at 11:54 PM. Six minutes to spare.

He backed the trailer into the dock with a surgeon's precision, the adrenaline still shaking his hands. He signed the digital manifest on the dock master's tablet.

"Cut it close, Turner," the dock master said, eyeing the scrape along the side of the trailer. "Looks like you kissed a wall."

"Just a love tap," Marcus wheezed, wiping cold sweat from his forehead. "Load's intact. Where's my bonus?"

The dock master tapped the screen. "Processing now. Load accepted. On-Time Bonus applied."

Marcus leaned back in the seat, closing his eyes. He had done it. He had beaten the storm, beaten the machine, and beaten the clock. He was the Last Bull.

A notification chimed on the dashboard.

*Payment Received: $3,600.00.*
*Network Access Fee (20%): -$720.00.*
*Weekly Loan & Sub Installment: -$1,725.00.*
*Insurance & Battery Surcharge: -$850.00.*
*Net Deposit: $305.00.*

Marcus stared at the screen.

Three hundred and five dollars.

He had risked his life. He had driven thirty-six hours. He had pulled off a miracle on black ice. And after the system took its cut, he had made less than a teenager flipping burgers.

But it wasn't zero.

It was enough for a steak. It was enough for a motel room with a hot shower. It was enough to lie to himself for another week—to believe that if he just kept the wheels spinning, the math would eventually stop chasing him.

"I won," he whispered to the empty cab.

But as he put the truck in park, he knew the truth. The machine didn't care if he lived or died. It only cared that he paid.
